# 112. Parameter Estimation in AR(1)

## What this covers

This chapter derives the likelihood for the AR(1) model $y_t = \phi_0 + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$
and asks whether its parameters can be estimated the way ordinary linear regression estimates
$\beta_0,\beta_1$. It assumes the AR($p$) model, ordinary linear regression and its Gaussian
likelihood, maximum likelihood estimation, and the Bayesian $t$-posterior for regression
coefficients (from an earlier lecture and homework) are already familiar. The payoff is two
competing definitions of "the AR(1) model" — one that simply fixes $y_1$ and never asks where it
came from, one that treats every $y_t$ as generated by an infinite history of past shocks — and a
first look at why $|\phi_1| < 1$ matters, ahead of a proper treatment of stationarity next lecture.

## From AR($p$) to a regression problem

Recall the AR($p$) model:
$$
y_t = \phi_0 + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \epsilon_t, \qquad t = p+1, \dots, n.
$$
Stacking the equations for $t = p+1,\dots,n$ gives the matrix form $Y = X\beta + \epsilon$, with
$$
Y = \begin{pmatrix} y_{p+1} \\ y_{p+2} \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{pmatrix}, \quad
X = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & y_p & y_{p-1} & \cdots & y_1 \\
1 & y_{p+1} & y_p & \cdots & y_2 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\
1 & y_{n-1} & y_{n-2} & \cdots & y_{n-p}
\end{pmatrix}, \quad
\beta = \begin{pmatrix} \phi_0 \\ \phi_1 \\ \vdots \\ \phi_p \end{pmatrix}, \quad
\epsilon = \begin{pmatrix} \epsilon_{p+1} \\ \epsilon_{p+2} \\ \vdots \\ \epsilon_n \end{pmatrix}.
$$
So AR($p$) is literally an instance of the ordinary linear regression model $Y = X\beta+\epsilon$ —
except that the "covariates" in $X$ and the "response" in $Y$ are both carved out of the *same*
single sequence $y_1,\dots,y_n$, rather than coming from independent observational units. That
single fact is what breaks the usual regression argument, and chasing down exactly where it breaks
is the point of this chapter. To keep the algebra light, take $p=1$ throughout and return to
general $p$ later:
$$
y_t = \phi_0 + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t, \qquad t = 2,\dots,n.
$$

## Detour: where does the regression likelihood actually come from?

The AR(1) equation looks exactly like a simple linear regression $y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i +
\epsilon_i$ with $i=t$, $x_i = y_{t-1}$. So before writing a likelihood for AR(1), it is worth
being precise about which assumptions produce the familiar Gaussian likelihood in *ordinary*
regression, so each one can be checked against the AR setting.

With data $(x_i,y_i)$, $i=1,\dots,m$, the likelihood is the joint density of the data, viewed as a
function of $\theta = (\beta_0,\beta_1,\sigma)$. Since the model expresses $y_i$ in terms of $x_i$,
it is natural to condition on the covariates first:
$$
f_{x_1,y_1,\dots,x_m,y_m\mid\theta} = f_{y_1,\dots,y_m\mid x_1,\dots,x_m,\theta}(y_1,\dots,y_m)\;
f_{x_1,\dots,x_m\mid\theta}(x_1,\dots,x_m).
$$
Substituting $y_i = \beta_0+\beta_1 x_i+\epsilon_i$ turns the first factor into a density of the
errors evaluated at the residuals $y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i$, still conditional on $x_1,\dots,
x_m,\theta$. From here, four separate assumptions are used, one at a time, to reach the familiar
likelihood:

1. **The model equation** $y_i = \beta_0+\beta_1 x_i + \epsilon_i$ itself.
2. **Independence of the errors and the covariates**: $\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_m$ independent of
   $x_1,\dots,x_m$ (given $\theta$). This is what lets the conditioning on $x_1,\dots,x_m$ be
   dropped from the error density.
3. $\epsilon_i \overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim} N(0,\sigma^2)$, which turns the error density into an
   explicit Gaussian product.
4. **The covariates' density does not depend on $\theta$**: $f_{x_1,\dots,x_m\mid\theta}$ is
   assumed free of $\theta$, so it is just a constant multiplicative factor and can be dropped when
   the likelihood is only needed up to proportionality.

Putting these together gives the familiar
$$
\text{Likelihood} \;\propto\; \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\right)^m
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^m (y_i-\beta_0-\beta_1 x_i)^2\right).
$$
From here, **frequentist** inference maximizes this to get
$\hat\beta = (X^TX)^{-1}X^Ty$ and $\hat\sigma_{\text{MLE}} = \sqrt{\text{RSS}/n}$, and then
separately works out the *sampling distribution* of $\hat\beta,\hat\sigma_{\text{MLE}}$ — using the
same four assumptions a second time. **Bayesian** inference instead combines the likelihood with
the prior $\beta_0,\beta_1,\log\sigma \overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}\text{unif}(-C,C)$ to get the
posterior
$$
\beta \mid \text{data} \sim t_{m-2,2}\!\left(\hat\beta, \hat\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}\right), \qquad
\hat\sigma := \sqrt{\text{RSS}/(m-2)},
$$
with a posterior for $\sigma$ derivable the same way (this was Homework One). The asymmetry to
notice: once the likelihood is written down, Bayesian inference never touches the four assumptions
again — only the likelihood function matters for the posterior. Frequentist inference uses the
assumptions **twice**: once to build the likelihood and the MLEs, and again to find the MLEs'
distribution. That asymmetry is exactly what will make AR(1) genuinely harder for one school than
the other.

## Why the same derivation fails for AR(1)

Check assumptions 2 and 4 against $y_t = \phi_0+\phi_1 y_{t-1}+\epsilon_t$ with $x_t = y_{t-1}$:

- **Assumption 4 fails outright.** The density of $x_t = y_{t-1}$ *does* depend on $\theta$,
  because $y_{t-1}$ is itself generated by the AR equation one step earlier,
  $y_{t-1} = \phi_0+\phi_1 y_{t-2}+\epsilon_{t-1}$ — so $\phi_0,\phi_1,\sigma$ all shape the
  distribution of $y_{t-1}$.
- **Assumption 2 is not obviously true either.** There is no a priori reason $\epsilon_t$ should be
  independent of $x_t = y_{t-1}, \dots$ (and, further back, of $y_1,\dots,y_{t-2}$), since those
  are earlier values of the very series $\epsilon_t$ helps generate.

So the regression argument cannot simply be recycled for AR(1). The likelihood has to be built
from a different route, using a different — but analogous — set of assumptions.

## Building the AR(1) likelihood by the chain rule

Rather than conditioning on covariates, factor the joint density of $y_1,\dots,y_n$ using the chain
rule for conditional densities:
$$
f_{y_1,\dots,y_n\mid\theta}(y_1,\dots,y_n) = f_{y_1\mid\theta}(y_1)\prod_{t=2}^n
f_{y_t\mid y_1,\dots,y_{t-1},\theta}(y_t).
$$
Substituting the model equation inside each conditional density turns
$f_{y_t\mid y_1,\dots,y_{t-1},\theta}(y_t)$ into the density of $\epsilon_t$ (conditional on the
past) evaluated at the residual $y_t - \phi_0-\phi_1 y_{t-1}$. Now assume $\epsilon_t$ is
independent of $y_1,\dots,y_{t-1}$ for each $t=2,\dots,n$ — the AR analogue of the regression
independence assumption, but stated about the driving noise rather than about covariates. This
removes the conditioning:
$$
f_{y_t\mid y_1,\dots,y_{t-1},\theta}(y_t) = f_{\epsilon_t}(y_t-\phi_0-\phi_1 y_{t-1}).
$$
With $\epsilon_t\overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)$,
$$
\text{Likelihood} = f_{y_1\mid\theta}(y_1)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\right)^{n-1}
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{t=2}^n(y_t-\phi_0-\phi_1 y_{t-1})^2\right). \tag{5}
$$
Only three assumptions went into this: the model equation, independence of $\epsilon_t$ from the
past, and $\epsilon_t$ i.i.d. normal. Notice what was *not* assumed: nothing at all was said about
the marginal distribution of $y_1$, because the model equation only constrains $t=2,\dots,n$ — it
never puts $y_1$ on the left-hand side. So $f_{y_1\mid\theta}(y_1)$ sits in (5) unresolved, and
what to do with it is the crux of the chapter.

## Approach one: ignore the distribution of $y_1$

The simplest fix is to assume $f_{y_1\mid\theta}(y_1)$ does not depend on $\theta$ at all. It then
becomes a constant factor, safe to drop since the likelihood is only needed up to proportionality:
$$
\text{Likelihood} \;\propto\; \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\right)^{n-1}
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{t=2}^n(y_t-\phi_0-\phi_1 y_{t-1})^2\right). \tag{6}
$$
This is *exactly* the ordinary-regression likelihood with $x_t=y_{t-1}$ — even though the route
that produced it (chain rule plus noise independent of the past) is different from the route that
produces the regression likelihood (covariate conditioning). Two consequences follow:

- **Bayesian inference is unchanged.** With the same prior $\phi_0,\phi_1,\log\sigma
  \overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}\text{unif}(-C,C)$, the posterior has exactly the regression form:
  $$
  (\phi_0,\phi_1)\mid y_1,\dots,y_n \sim t_{n-3,2}\!\left((\hat\phi_0,\hat\phi_1),
  \hat\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}\right),
  $$
  where $\hat\phi_0,\hat\phi_1$ are just the least-squares estimates from regressing $y_t$ on
  $y_{t-1}$. The degrees of freedom are $n-3$, not $n-2$: the effective sample size is $n-1$ (since
  $t$ runs from $2$ to $n$) and $X$ has $2$ columns, so $(n-1)-2 = n-3$.
- **Frequentist inference is not.** The MLEs of $\phi_0,\phi_1,\sigma$ under (6) coincide with the
  OLS estimates, since (6) *is* the regression likelihood. But finding the *sampling distribution*
  of those MLEs is a different problem than in ordinary regression, because $x_t=y_{t-1}$ is here
  a random quantity correlated with the rest of the series, not a fixed covariate. This has to be
  worked out asymptotically, as $n\to\infty$, using central limit theorems and laws of large
  numbers for *dependent* sequences (see Shumway and Stoffer, *Time Series Analysis and Its
  Applications*, 4th ed., Ch. 4 — referred to in the lecture but not derived there). The resulting
  inference is based on normal approximations, not exact $t$-distributions.

This is the first place Bayesian and frequentist inference genuinely diverge for AR models:
Bayesian inference stays exact and no harder than ordinary regression, while frequentist inference
needs asymptotic machinery just to describe the MLE's distribution. This is also why software that
actually fits autoregressions — `AutoReg` in `statsmodels`, for instance — reports standard errors
and $z$-scores rather than the $t$-scores a plain OLS regression of $y_t$ on $y_{t-1}$ would give:
it is using the frequentist asymptotic formulas, not (6) treated as an exact regression likelihood.
The two differ in principle, though usually the difference is negligible in practice.

## Approach two: derive the distribution of $y_1$

A more careful fix is to work out what $f_{y_1\mid\theta}(y_1)$ actually should be, by imagining
the model equation extended backward to times before the sample starts: $t = 1,0,-1,-2,\dots$.
Substituting recursively,
$$
\begin{aligned}
y_1 &= \phi_0+\phi_1 y_0+\epsilon_1 \\
    &= \phi_0(1+\phi_1) + \phi_1^2 y_{-1} + \phi_1\epsilon_0+\epsilon_1 \\
    &= \phi_0(1+\phi_1+\phi_1^2) + \phi_1^3 y_{-2} + \phi_1^2\epsilon_{-1}+\phi_1\epsilon_0+
       \epsilon_1,
\end{aligned}
$$
and continuing this way down to $t=-M$ for some large $M$,
$$
y_1 = \phi_0\sum_{j=0}^M \phi_1^j + \phi_1^{M+1}y_{-M} + \sum_{j=0}^M\phi_1^j\epsilon_{1-j}.
$$
This is not yet a distribution for $y_1$, since $y_{-M}$ is unknown. But if $|\phi_1|<1$, the
coefficient $\phi_1^{M+1}$ shrinks geometrically as $M$ grows, so for large $M$ that term
contributes almost nothing regardless of $y_{-M}$. Dropping it and letting $M\to\infty$,
$$
y_1 \approx \phi_0\sum_{j=0}^\infty \phi_1^j + \sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^j\epsilon_{1-j}
= \frac{\phi_0}{1-\phi_1} + \sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^j\epsilon_{1-j}.
$$

The remaining sum is an infinite sum of independent normal random variables, hence itself normal,
with mean $0$ (each $\epsilon_{1-j}$ has mean $0$) and variance
$$
\text{var}\left(\sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^j\epsilon_{1-j}\right) =
\sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^{2j}\,\text{var}(\epsilon_{1-j}) = \sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^{2j} =
\frac{\sigma^2}{1-\phi_1^2},
$$
using $|\phi_1|<1$ so the geometric series converges. Hence
$$
y_1 \sim N\!\left(\frac{\phi_0}{1-\phi_1}, \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\phi_1^2}\right), \qquad
f_{y_1\mid\theta}(y_1) = \frac{\sqrt{1-\phi_1^2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\exp\left(-\frac{1-\phi_1^2}{2\sigma^2}\left(y_1-\frac{\phi_0}{1-\phi_1}\right)^2\right).
$$
Plugging this into (5) gives the full likelihood
$$
\text{Likelihood} = \frac{\sqrt{1-\phi_1^2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\exp\left(-\frac{1-\phi_1^2}{2\sigma^2}\left(y_1-\frac{\phi_0}{1-\phi_1}\right)^2\right)
\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\right)^{n-1}
\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{t=2}^n(y_t-\phi_0-\phi_1 y_{t-1})^2\right). \tag{7}
$$
This is more complicated than (6), and it is valid only when $|\phi_1|<1$ — outside that range the
geometric series it rests on simply does not converge, and the whole derivation collapses.

## Two different AR(1) models

The two approaches to $f_{y_1\mid\theta}(y_1)$ are not just two computational shortcuts to the same
target — they amount to two different definitions of what "the AR(1) model" *is*.

**Model A** (conditional model, likelihood (6)):
$$
y_t = \phi_0+\phi_1 y_{t-1}+\epsilon_t \ \ (t=2,\dots,n), \qquad
\epsilon_t \text{ independent of } y_1,\dots,y_{t-1}, \qquad
\epsilon_t\overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2),
$$
with $y_1$ simply treated as a fixed constant — its distribution is never modelled.

**Model B** (stationary model, likelihood (7)): take the backward expansion to its limit and define,
for *every* integer $t$ (not just $t=2,\dots,n$),
$$
y_t = \frac{\phi_0}{1-\phi_1} + \sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^j\epsilon_{t-j}, \qquad
t = \dots,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\dots, \tag{8}
$$
with $\epsilon_t\overset{\text{i.i.d}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma^2)$. Substituting $y_1$'s formula into
$y_2 = \phi_0+\phi_1 y_1+\epsilon_2$ gives
$$
y_2 = \phi_0+\phi_1\left(\frac{\phi_0}{1-\phi_1}+\sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^j\epsilon_{1-j}\right)+
\epsilon_2 = \frac{\phi_0}{1-\phi_1}+\sum_{j=0}^\infty\phi_1^j\epsilon_{2-j},
$$
i.e. exactly formula (8) shifted to $t=2$; induction extends this to every $t$. So (8) is
self-consistent: it automatically satisfies the AR equation $y_t=\phi_0+\phi_1 y_{t-1}+\epsilon_t$,
and automatically makes $\epsilon_t$ independent of $y_{t-1},y_{t-2},\dots$ — both of the
assumptions that Model A had to state separately fall out of the single formula (8) for free.

Model B is only well-defined when $|\phi_1|<1$: the infinite sum $\sum_j\phi_1^j\epsilon_{t-j}$
fails to converge when $|\phi_1|\ge1$, so the right-hand side of (8) makes no sense outside that
range. Model B is the chapter's first example of a **stationary** model — the same distributional
formula generates every $y_t$, with no special role for a starting time — and working out exactly
what stationarity buys, and requires, is the subject of the next lecture.
