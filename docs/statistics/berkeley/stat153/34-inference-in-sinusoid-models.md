# 34. Inference in Sinusoid Models

## What this covers

This chapter works through Code Lab 3 of the course: data are generated by a single sinusoid of
unknown frequency buried in noise, and the question is how to estimate that frequency, attach an
uncertainty statement to the estimate, and understand why some frequencies are indistinguishable
from others once you sample at equally spaced times. It assumes ordinary least squares and its MLE
interpretation, the basic mechanics of Bayesian inference (prior times likelihood gives a posterior,
up to a normalizing constant), and the $\chi^2$ and multivariate normal distributions used when
sampling from a Bayesian linear model.

## The sinusoid regression model

The model is
$$y_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1\cos(2\pi f t) + \beta_2 \sin(2\pi f t) + \epsilon_t, \qquad t=1,\dots,n,$$
with $\epsilon_t$ iid noise and $\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2,f,\sigma$ all unknown. The lab works with a
simulated instance: $n=400$, true $f=0.2035$, $\beta=(0,3,5)$, $\sigma=10$.

The structural fact the rest of the lab exploits: for a *fixed* value of $f$, the design matrix
$X_f = [\mathbf 1,\ \cos(2\pi f t),\ \sin(2\pi f t)]$ is completely determined, and the model is an
ordinary linear regression in $\beta$. The only genuinely nonlinear parameter is $f$, since it sits
inside the cosine and sine. That turns a nonlinear least-squares problem in four parameters into a
one-dimensional search over $f$, with $\beta$ profiled out by OLS at each candidate value.

## Estimating the frequency by profile least squares

Define the profile residual sum of squares
$$RSS(f) = \min_{\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2} \sum_{t=1}^n \Big(y_t - \beta_0 - \beta_1\cos(2\pi f t) -
\beta_2\sin(2\pi f t)\Big)^2,$$
computed by fitting an OLS regression for each candidate $f$ and summing squared residuals:
```python
def rss(f):
    xcos = np.cos(2*np.pi*f*t)
    xsin = np.sin(2*np.pi*f*t)
    X = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), xcos, xsin])
    md = sm.OLS(y, X).fit()
    return np.sum(md.resid**2)
```
$RSS(f)$ is not convex in $f$ — it oscillates, with local minima at spurious frequencies that
happen to fit part of the sinusoid — so the lab does not use gradient-based optimization. Instead it
evaluates $RSS(f)$ on a dense grid of $100{,}000$ points over $f\in[0,\tfrac12]$ (the restriction to
$[0,\tfrac12]$ is explained below) and takes $\hat f = \arg\min_f RSS(f)$. On the simulated data this
gives $\hat f = 0.20355$, matching the generating value $f = 0.2035$ to four digits.

## Estimating the remaining parameters

Once $f$ is fixed at $\hat f$, the model is a genuine linear regression, and the remaining
parameters are estimated exactly as in ordinary least squares:

- $\hat\beta$ is the OLS coefficient vector of $y$ on $X_{\hat f}$ — already computed inside `rss`;
- the MLE of $\sigma^2$ is $\widehat{\sigma^2}_{\mathrm{MLE}} = RSS(\hat f)/n$ (biased downward, as
  always for OLS);
- the unbiased estimator divides by the residual degrees of freedom instead,
  $\widehat{\sigma^2} = RSS(\hat f)/(n-p)$ with $p=3$ parameters in $\beta$.

On the simulated data: $\hat\beta = (-0.053,\ 3.004,\ 5.639)$ against the true $(0,3,5)$, and
$\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{MLE}}=9.50$, $\hat\sigma_{\mathrm{unbiased}}=9.54$ against the true
$\sigma=10$ — recovered reasonably well from a single noisy realization.

## A Bayesian posterior for the frequency

Point estimates say nothing about how confident to be in $\hat f$. The lab quantifies that with a
posterior for $f$:
$$p(f\mid \text{data}) \ \propto\ \mathbb 1\{0\le f\le \tfrac12\}\ \big|X_f^\top X_f\big|^{-1/2}
\left(\frac{1}{RSS(f)}\right)^{(n-p)/2}.$$
This is the marginal posterior of $f$ once $\beta$ and $\sigma$ have been integrated out under a
flat/reference prior; that derivation belongs to the main lecture on Bayesian linear models and is
not repeated in the lab — here the formula is the starting point for computation.

### Working on the log scale

With $n=400$, the exponent $(n-p)/2\approx 198$, so $RSS(f)^{-(n-p)/2}$ overflows or underflows in
floating point. The lab instead computes
$$\log p(f\mid\text{data}) = \frac{p-n}{2}\log RSS(f) \;-\; \tfrac12 \log\big|X_f^\top X_f\big| +
\text{const},$$
using `np.linalg.slogdet` for a numerically safe log-determinant. There is a second hazard at the
boundary: as $f\to 0$ or $f\to\tfrac12$, the columns of $X_f$ become collinear (the sine column
vanishes as $f\to 0$, for instance), so $X_f^\top X_f$ becomes near-singular and
$|X_f^\top X_f|^{-1/2}$ blows up. The grid used for the posterior avoids the immediate neighborhood
of $0$ and $\tfrac12$ — the lab zooms in on $f\in[0.05,0.35]$, informed by the earlier
RSS-versus-frequency curve (the lab calls this "the periodogram") showing the maximizing frequency
near $0.2$.

The log posterior traces a curve similar in shape to that periodogram — both peak at the same
frequencies — but once exponentiated the posterior has essentially one dominant peak: the exponent
$(n-p)/2$ that separates the true frequency's tiny $RSS$ from every competing frequency's larger
$RSS$ is large enough, at $n=400$, to swamp every secondary peak.

## Credible intervals from a grid

With the posterior evaluated and normalized to sum to 1 on an equally spaced grid, a $95\%$
credible interval is built by growing a symmetric window around the posterior mode until it
captures $95\%$ of the mass:
```python
def PostProbAroundMax(m):
    est_ind = np.argmax(postvals)
    return np.sum(postvals[(est_ind-m):(est_ind+m)])

m = 0
while PostProbAroundMax(m) <= 0.95:
    m += 1
```
Since the posterior is unimodal away from the boundary singularities, a symmetric window around the
mode stands in for a highest-posterior-density interval. On the simulated data this gives $m=10$
grid steps either side of the mode: $\hat f = 0.20356$ with $95\%$ credible interval
$(0.20326,\ 0.20386)$ — matching the posterior mean ($0.20355$), the posterior mode ($0.20356$),
and the least-squares estimate ($0.20355$) to several digits.

## Posterior sampling for the whole parameter vector

To visualize uncertainty in the *fitted curve*, not just in $f$, the lab draws joint posterior
samples of $(f,\sigma,\beta)$ by composition — draw $f$, then $\sigma$ given $f$, then $\beta$
given $(f,\sigma)$:

1. **Sample $f$** from the discretized posterior: `rng.choice(allfvals, N, p=postvals)`, treating
   the normalized grid values as a discrete approximation to the continuous posterior.
2. **Sample $\sigma$ given $f$**, using the pivotal fact (proved in Problem 5 of Homework 1, not
   re-derived here) that
   $$\frac{RSS(f)}{\sigma^2}\ \Big|\ \text{data}, f \ \sim\ \chi^2_{n-p},$$
   so a draw $\chi\sim\chi^2_{n-p}$ gives the posterior draw $\sigma = \sqrt{RSS(f)/\chi}$.
3. **Sample $\beta$ given $(f,\sigma)$** from
   $$\beta \mid \text{data}, \sigma, f \ \sim\ N_3\!\big(\hat\beta_f,\ \sigma^2 (X_f^\top
   X_f)^{-1}\big),$$
   the same covariance shape as the sampling distribution of the OLS estimator, used here as a
   conditional posterior.

Repeating this $N=2000$ times and overlaying the resulting fitted curves
$\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1\cos(2\pi f t)+\hat\beta_2\sin(2\pi f t)$ on the raw data gives a band of
plausible curves — a direct visualization of parameter uncertainty rather than a single best fit.
Summarizing the 2000 draws recovers the generating parameters well: posterior means
$(\hat f,\hat\beta_0,\hat\beta_1,\hat\beta_2,\hat\sigma) \approx (0.2036,\ -0.048,\ 2.93,\ 5.49,\
9.57)$ against the true $(0.2035,\ 0,\ 3,\ 5,\ 10)$.

## Frequency aliasing

Now suppose the data are generated instead by $f = 2-0.2035$, still sampled at the equally spaced
integer times $t=1,\dots,n$. The frequency estimate produced by the same procedure is still
$\approx 0.2035$ — the two frequencies are **aliases** of one another. For integer $t$,
$$\cos\big(2\pi(2-f)t\big) = \cos(4\pi t - 2\pi f t) = \cos(-2\pi f t) = \cos(2\pi f t),$$
$$\sin\big(2\pi(2-f)t\big) = \sin(4\pi t - 2\pi f t) = \sin(-2\pi f t) = -\sin(2\pi f t),$$
using that $4\pi t$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$, and that $\cos$ and $\sin$ are $2\pi$-periodic.
So a sinusoid at frequency $2-f$ agrees exactly with one at frequency $f$ on every integer sample,
with the sine term flipping sign. No amount of data collected at equally spaced integer times can
distinguish $f$ from $2-f$; correspondingly, fitting the model at the "wrong" alias recovers the
true $\beta_0,\beta_1$ but a sign-flipped estimate of $\beta_2$, exactly as the identity predicts.

### Breaking aliasing with unequally spaced samples

The aliasing identity uses $t$ integer in an essential way — $4\pi t$ is a multiple of $2\pi$ only
then. If the sampling times are not equally spaced, that cancellation no longer happens, and
frequencies outside $[0,\tfrac12]$ become identifiable again. The lab demonstrates this by
regenerating the data with $f = 2-0.2035\approx 1.80$, but with $t$ drawn as $n$ sorted uniform
points on $[1,n]$ rather than the integers $1,\dots,n$. Repeating the same profile-RSS search, now
over the wider grid $f\in[0,10]$ — there is no longer a reason to restrict to $[0,\tfrac12]$ — gives
$\hat f = 1.7965$, matching the true value directly with no aliasing. The Bayesian posterior
(computed the same way, again on the log scale and again excluding a neighborhood of $f=0$ to avoid
the same near-singularity as before) concentrates tightly around $1.7965$ as well, with posterior
mean $1.79654$ and mode $1.79652$.
