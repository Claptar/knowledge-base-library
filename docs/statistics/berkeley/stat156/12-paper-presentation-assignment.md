# 12. Paper Presentation Assignment

## What this covers

This chapter is not a lecture. It reproduces the course's paper-presentation assignment, one of
Stat 156/256's two graded pieces of work: each student records a video presenting two published
causal-inference papers. It is kept here, rather than dropped, because a reader who has worked
through this book has the tools to attempt the same exercise on a paper of their own choosing.

## The assignment

You present two published papers, each in its own recorded video presentation of about 20 minutes.
The goal is twofold: to engage thoughtfully with academic research in causal inference, and to hone
presentation skills. Use slides or a virtual whiteboard to carry notation, mathematical expressions,
graphs, and figures — the talk should stand on its own as a piece of exposition, not just a reading
of the paper.

## What counts as an admissible paper

- The paper must be related to causal inference.
- It must be published after 2012.
- At least one of the two papers must be a journal paper (as opposed to a conference paper).

## Recommended venues

Journals: *Annals of Statistics*, *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, *Journal of
the Royal Statistical Society* Series A, B, and C, *Biometrika*, *Biometrics*, *Annals of Applied
Statistics*, *Econometrica*, *Journal of Econometrics*, *Data & Policy*, *Journal of Machine
Learning Research*, *PNAS*, *Science*, *Nature*.

Conferences: AISTATS, ICML, NeurIPS, and the ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and
Transparency (FAccT).

## Grading criteria

Presentations were evaluated on three criteria, in the course's own terms:

1. **Content.** Clearly describe and motivate the problem setting, and situate the research in the
   context of prior work. Spend a significant portion of the talk on the paper's proposed
   contribution, both conceptually and in mathematical detail, and report any empirical findings.
   Engage critically with the paper: offer your own commentary on its novelty, strengths,
   weaknesses, and significance.
2. **Presentation.** The talk should be accessible and informative to someone who has not read the
   paper but has taken the causal inference course. Emphasize the key aspects of the research, give
   intuition or motivation where they are complex or non-obvious, define new terms, use consistent
   language, and pace the talk appropriately.
3. **Relevance.** The paper must meet the admissibility criteria above — causal inference,
   published after 2012, and so on. A paper that does not loses points on this criterion regardless
   of how well it is presented.
