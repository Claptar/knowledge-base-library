# 13. Paper Presentation Guidelines

## What this covers

This chapter records the requirements for the paper-presentation assignment in the causal
inference course: which papers qualify, how the presentation itself should be built and
delivered, and how it is graded. It assumes only that the reader is taking the course and has
followed its treatment of causal inference — the assignment does not introduce new
causal-inference content, and neither does this chapter.

## The assignment

You present two published papers, each in its own recorded video, at two points in the term: the
first presentation is due October 15, the second November 14.

The assignment serves two purposes at once — engaging seriously with research in causal inference,
and practising the separate skill of presenting that research well. Each recording should run
about 20 minutes per paper. You should use slides or a virtual whiteboard to carry the notation,
the mathematical expressions, and any graphs or figures the paper relies on — a presentation about
a technical paper that stays purely verbal is not going to convey the argument.

Submission is direct: email the recording to the instructor. Posting it on your own website or on
YouTube afterward is welcome but optional, never required.

## Choosing a paper

Both papers must be about causal inference and published after 2012. At least one of the two must
be a journal paper — the other could come from a strong conference instead.

Recommended journals: *Annals of Statistics*, *Journal of the American Statistical Association*,
*Journal of the Royal Statistical Society* Series A, B, and C, *Biometrika*, *Annals of Applied
Statistics*, *Biometrics*, *Econometrica*, *Journal of Econometrics*, *Data & Policy*, *Journal of
Machine Learning Research*, *PNAS*, *Science*, *Nature*.

Recommended venues outside the journal literature: AISTATS, ICML, NeurIPS, and the ACM Conference
on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT).

## Grading criteria

The presentation is judged on three things.

**Content.** Describe and motivate the problem the paper addresses, and place it against prior
work rather than treating it as if it appeared out of nowhere. The bulk of the time should go to
the paper's actual contribution, covered both conceptually and in mathematical detail — not just
"the paper proposes X" but what X is and why it works. Report the empirical findings too, where
the paper has any. And go beyond exposition: offer your own assessment of the paper's novelty,
its strengths and weaknesses, and how significant the contribution actually is.

**Presentation.** Pitch the talk at someone who has taken the causal inference course but has not
read the paper. That audience already has the course's background, so the job is to carry them
from that baseline to the paper's specific contribution — flagging what is genuinely subtle or
non-obvious and giving the intuition for it, rather than presenting every step as equally routine.
Define any new terms as you introduce them, keep the notation consistent through the talk, and
pace it so the material doesn't feel rushed at the end. The instructions add, plainly: have fun
with it, and the audience will stay engaged.

**Relevance.** The paper actually has to satisfy the eligibility rules above — causal inference,
published after 2012, and so on. A paper that is interesting but off-topic or too old costs points
here regardless of how well it is presented.
