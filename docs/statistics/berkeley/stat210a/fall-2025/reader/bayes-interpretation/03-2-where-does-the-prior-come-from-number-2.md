# 2 Where Does the Prior Come From? {number="2"}

**Source:** [`reader/bayes-interpretation.html`](https://github.com/berkeley-stat210a/fall-2025/blob/5eb849a4924c34eb73e098cdfc812ffa8d806501/reader/bayes-interpretation.html) · **Licence:** CC BY 4.0 · Converted 2026-09-14 from `.html` (good)

## <span class="header-section-number">2.1</span> Evading the problem of induction {.anchored number="2.1" anchor-id="evading-the-problem-of-induction"}

We discussed in [Lecture 1](../introduction/index.md) that there are, broadly speaking, two ways that we can evade the problem of induction. So far in this semester, we have focused on the evasion of *inductive behavior*: as statistical methodologists, we can devise methods for estimation, testing, and other decision problems and analyze these methods’ MSEs, or bound their Type I error rates, treating <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> as an unknown quantity that can vary freely over the parameter space (and is not necessarily governed by a prior). A frequentist is willing to present an estimator, prove that it is highly precise (say, that it’s off by <span class="math inline">\$0.1\$</span> no more than <span class="math inline">\$1\\%\$</span> of the time), and then use it to calculate an estimate <span class="math inline">\$\\delta(X)\$</span> for <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> on a given data set <span class="math inline">\$X\$</span>. But if you ask them whether we can say that <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> is therefore probably close to the estimate they just calculated, they may deny that the question even makes sense. <span class="math inline">\$X\$</span> has already been realized so there is no more probability left in the problem to talk about; the probability statement they were making before was only with respect to its distribution.

If frequentist methods evade the problem of induction by answering a different question, Bayesian methods evade it by begging the question. In the Bayesian paradigm, we can use the posterior distribution to freely make statements about the posterior distribution of <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span>. The only trouble is that these statements follow from an assumption we made, before seeing the data, about the prior distribution of <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span>. So the Bayesian must be prepared to answer questions about where the prior comes from.[^1]

## <span class="header-section-number">2.2</span> Subjective priors {.anchored number="2.2" anchor-id="subjective-priors"}

If we take the subjectivist view, coming up with a prior boils down to introspection about what our prior beliefs actually are. For example, if we introspect about our subjective beliefs about bias of a new coin we encounter, our subjective prior might look roughly like the stylized picture below, placing most of the mass close to <span class="math inline">\$0.5\$</span>, but reserving some subjective probability that the coin is a trick coin that is either going to land heads or tails with probability close to <span class="math inline">\$1\$</span>, or has some other wildcard distribution. The picture below is a mixture of a <span class="math inline">\$\\text{Beta}(400,400)\$</span> (the central bump), a <span class="math inline">\$\\text{Beta}(0.1,0.1)\$</span> (the spikes at the extremes), and <span class="math inline">\$\\text{Beta}(1,1)=\\text{Unif}$$0,1$$\$</span> (wildcard option to cover all bases), with <span class="math inline">\$90\\%\$</span>, <span class="math inline">\$15\\%\$</span>, and <span class="math inline">\$5\\%\$</span> prior mass on each theory (perhaps we are slightly suspicious of the person presenting us with the coin).

This approach has several advantages. First, if the prior reflects our beliefs before seeing the data, then the posterior has the philosophically straightforward interpretation of reflecting our beliefs after seeing the data. Another benefit of the approach is that, as we have seen, it can bring real information to bear on the problem in a useful way.

However, the approach can be difficult to carry out in practice. We saw this with the nonparametric example above, but similar issues arise in parametric problems with a large number of parameters: what is your subjective joint prior distribution over the effects of one million single nucleotide polymorphisms on a person’s likelihood of suffering from diabetes?

The subjectivity of the answer we obtain through this approach is not an issue in some contexts (for example, if we are running a business) but it has limited the application of subjective Bayesian statistics in scientific endeavors. It is embarrassing to write in the abstract that “in my opinion, the mass of the Higgs boson is between…”

In response to this, some practitioners look for “objective” rules for selection of the prior:

## <span class="header-section-number">2.3</span> Objective or vague priors {.anchored number="2.3" anchor-id="objective-or-vague-priors"}

If we prefer not to have our subjective beliefs influence the result, we might pick a prior that implements a kind of “principle of indifference,” meaning that before we see any data we should give equal weight to all possibilities. This is simple to implement when the parameter space <span class="math inline">\$\\Theta\$</span> is finite, but for the more common case of a continuous parameter space, it is less clear what it means to assign equal probability to all parameter values. Indeed, *any* continuous prior would assign zero probability to every individual value.

### <span class="header-section-number">2.3.1</span> Flat prior {.anchored number="2.3.1" anchor-id="flat-prior"}

Instead, we can try to spread probability around equally to equally sized neighborhoods. One popular choice of objective prior is the flat prior, which uses a uniform density on <span class="math inline">\$\\Theta\$</span>. If <span class="math inline">\$\\Theta\$</span> is not bounded, we can still use <span class="math inline">\$\\lambda(\\theta) = 1\$</span> as an *improper* prior, meaning it is non-normalizable. Even though the prior is not normalizable, it is commonly the case that the posterior will nevertheless be normalizable because the likelihood is: for example, consider the simple problem <span class="math display">\\$$ \\begin{aligned} \\theta &\\sim \\lambda(\\theta) = 1\\\\ X\\mid \\theta &\\sim N(\\theta,\\sigma^2) = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2\\pi}}e^{-(x-\\theta)^2/2\\sigma^2} \\end{aligned} \\$$</span> In this problem, if we ignore the fact that the prior is improper and just chug through the calculations, we get <span class="math display">\\$$ \\lambda(\\theta \\mid x) \\propto\_\\theta \\lambda(\\theta) p\_\\theta(x) = \\frac{1}{\\sqrt{2\\pi}}e^{-(\\theta-x)^2/2\\sigma^2} = N(x,\\sigma^2), \\$$</span> which is a normalizable posterior.

### <span class="header-section-number">2.3.2</span> Jeffreys prior {.anchored number="2.3.2" anchor-id="jeffreys-prior"}

The flat prior spreads mass evenly across the parameter space, but that makes it dependent on how we parameterize our model.

**Example: Different parameterizations of a binomial model**

Consider a <span class="math inline">\$\\text{Binom}(n,\\theta)\$</span> model, and suppose that we place a flat prior on the probability parameter <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span>. The resulting prior on the natural parameter <span class="math inline">\$\\eta(\\theta) = \\log\\frac{\\theta}{1-\\theta}\$</span> is obtained by the change of variables formula with inverse <span class="math inline">\$\\theta(\\eta) = \\frac{e^{\\eta}}{1+e^\\eta}\$</span>: <span class="math display">\\$$ \\lambda^{(\\eta)}(\\eta) = \|\\dot\\theta(\\eta)\|\\lambda^{(\\theta)}(\\theta(\\eta)) = \\frac{e^{\\eta}}{(1+e^{\\eta})^2}. \\$$</span> That is, the prior density tends to zero as <span class="math inline">\$\|\\eta\|\$</span> tends to <span class="math inline">\$\\infty\$</span>. Intuitively, the portion of our model with <span class="math inline">\$\\eta\\in $$-11,-10$$\$</span> is the same as the portion with <span class="math inline">\$\\theta \\in $$1.7\\times 10^{-5}, 4.5\\times 10^{-5}$$\$</span>, which is assigned virtually no prior mass.

By contrast, if we place a (non-normalizable) flat prior on <span class="math inline">\$\\eta\$</span>, we end up with an improper prior on <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> that is proportional to <span class="math inline">\$\\frac{1}{\\theta(1-\\theta)}\$</span>, which we can think of as the limit of a <span class="math inline">\$\\text{Beta}(\\alpha,\\alpha)\$</span> distribution with <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\\to 0\$</span>. As a prior on <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span>, this effectively places very high mass at the extremes of the parameter space — again intuitively, because the very small interval <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\\in $$0.0001,0.01$$\$</span> corresponds to an interval of length roughly <span class="math inline">\$4.6 \\approx \\log(100)\$</span> in the natural parameter space.

To address this issue, we can instead use the **Jeffreys prior**, which tries to spread the prior mass evenly over our statistical model in a way that is invariant to the parameterization. The Jeffreys prior in a given parameterization is the (possibly improper) prior that is proportional to <span class="math inline">\$\|J(\\theta)\|^{1/2}\$</span>, where <span class="math inline">\$\|J(\\theta)\|\$</span> is the determinant of the Jacobian: <span class="math display">\\$$ \\lambda\_{\\text{Jeff}}(\\theta) \\propto\_\\theta \|J(\\theta)\|^{1/2} \\$$</span> The Jeffreys prior is invariant to smooth re-parameterization of the model and, it can be shown (HW 6), corresponds to assigning equal prior mass to small neighborhoods of the same size, as measured by the KL divergence.

**Example, Continued.** In the binomial model, the Fisher information with respect to the natural parameter is <span class="math inline">\$J^{(\\eta)}(\\eta) = \\Var\_\\eta(X)\$</span>, so the Fisher information with respect to <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> is <span class="math display">\\$$ \\dot\\eta(\\theta)^2 \\Var\_{\\theta}(X) = \\left(\\theta(1-\\theta)\\right)^{-2}\\cdot n\\theta(1-\\theta) = \\frac{n}{\\theta(1-\\theta)} \\$$</span> Thus, the Jeffreys prior on <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> is <span class="math display">\\$$ \\lambda\_{\\text{Jeff}}^{(\\theta)}(\\theta) \\propto\_\\theta \\sqrt{\\frac{1}{\\theta(1-\\theta)}} \\propto\_\\theta \\text{Beta}\\left(\\frac{1}{2},\\frac{1}{2}\\right). \\$$</span> If we carry out the same calculation for the natural parameter, the Fisher information is <span class="math display">\\$$ J(\\eta) = \\Var\_\\eta(X) =\\theta(\\eta)(1-\\theta(\\eta)) = n\\frac{e^{\\eta}}{(1+e^{\\eta})^2}, \\$$</span> so the Jeffreys prior is <span class="math display">\\$$ \\lambda\_{\\text{Jeff}}^{(\\eta)}(\\eta) \\propto\_\\eta \\sqrt{\\frac{e^{\\eta}}{(1+e^{\\eta})^2}}. \\$$</span> These two priors are the same: applying the change of variables formula to <span class="math inline">\$\\lambda\_{\\text{Jeff}}^{(\\theta)}(\\theta)\$</span> gives <span class="math display">\\$$ \\lambda^{(\\eta)}(\\eta) = \|\\dot\\theta(\\eta)\|\\lambda\_{\\text{Jeff}}^{(\\theta)}(\\theta(\\eta)) = \\sqrt{\\frac{e^{\\eta}}{(1+e^{\\eta})^2}} \\$$</span>

### <span class="header-section-number">2.3.3</span> Drawbacks of vague priors {.anchored number="2.3.3" anchor-id="drawbacks-of-vague-priors"}

That vague priors can sometimes be improper can be a drawback. When we use an improper prior, we will not necessarily retain all of the decision-theoretic advantages of a proper prior, such as admissibility (see HW 5). For example, we can see this by returning to the example we examined when we raised doubts about unbiasedness

**Example:** We observe <span class="math inline">\$X \\sim N\_d(\\mu, I\_d)\$</span> for <span class="math inline">\$\\mu \\in \\RR^d\$</span>, and we want to estimate <span class="math inline">\$\\rho^2 = g(\\mu) = \\\|\\mu\\\|^2\$</span>. If we use a flat prior (or a Jeffreys prior, which amounts to the same thing in this case), the posterior is again just the likelihood <span class="math inline">\$\\lambda(\\mu \\mid x) = N\_d(x,I\_d)\$</span>. The posterior mean of <span class="math inline">\$\\mu\$</span> is <span class="math inline">\$\\EE$$\\mu \\mid X$$ = X\$</span>, which is a very reasonable estimator, but the posterior mean of <span class="math inline">\$\\rho^2\$</span> is much more questionable: <span class="math display">\\$$ \\delta\_\\lambda(X) = \\EE\[\\\|\\mu\\\|^2 \\mid X$$ = \\\|X\\\|^2 + d. \\\]</span> Recalling that the UMVU estimator was <span class="math inline">\$\\\|X\\\|^2 - d\$</span>, we may be disturbed to find that we have added a bias of <span class="math inline">\$2d\$</span> without changing the variance at all, so <span class="math inline">\$\\text{MSE}(\\mu; \\delta\_\\lambda) = \\text{MSE}(\\mu; \\delta\_{\\text{UMVU}}(X)) + 4d^2\$</span>. This penalty could be far larger than the variance of <span class="math inline">\$\\\|X\\\|^2\$</span>, which is <span class="math inline">\$O(d)\$</span> for small values of <span class="math inline">\$\\mu\$</span>.

Why did we get such a bad estimator? One way to see this is by examining the effective prior on <span class="math inline">\$\\rho\$</span>: for small <span class="math inline">\$\\ep &gt; 0\$</span>, the event that <span class="math inline">\$\\\|\\mu\\\| \\in $$r,r+\\ep$$\$</span> is a spherical shell of width <span class="math inline">\$\\ep\$</span>, whose volume is proportional to <span class="math inline">\$r^{d-1}\\ep\$</span>, so the probability density grows rapidly in <span class="math inline">\$r\$</span>. Because there are many more values of <span class="math inline">\$\\mu\$</span> with large <span class="math inline">\$\\\|\\mu\\\|\$</span> than with small <span class="math inline">\$\\\|\\mu\\\|\$</span>, the prior puts much more mass on them, and this pushes up our estimator.

Another conceptual difficulty comes when we try to interpret the posterior. If the prior was no one’s subjective belief before seeing the data, the posterior is also no one’s subjective belief after seeing the data. But then, what is it?

### <span class="header-section-number">2.3.4</span> Intersubjective Agreement {.anchored number="2.3.4" anchor-id="intersubjective-agreement"}

Flat priors are especially attractive in cases when the prior really doesn’t matter. For example, when we have a lot of data in a relatively low-dimensional parameter space, the data may effectively rule out most <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> values because the likelihood is extremely low outside of a relatively small region of the parameter space. A somewhat extreme example of this type is the coin flipping data we saw in [Lecture 1](../introduction/index.md) where <span class="math inline">\$n=350,757\$</span> and <span class="math inline">\$X=178,079\$</span>, where the likelihood is proportional to <span class="math inline">\$\\theta^{178,079}(1-\\theta)^{172,678}\$</span>, a function with an extremely narrow peak near <span class="math inline">\$X/n\\approx 0.508\$</span>.

## <span class="header-section-number">2.4</span> Convenience priors {.anchored number="2.4" anchor-id="convenience-priors"}

Because Bayesian inference is often computation-heavy, one important practical goal in choosing a prior is computational tractability. In most Bayesian models, the priors used are exponential families, often ones that are conjugate to the likelihood.

One justification people sometimes use for choosing exponential priors relates to a kind of generalization of the principle of indifference called the **maximum entropy principle**. The entropy of a density relative to some base measure <span class="math inline">\$\\mu\$</span> on a sample space <span class="math inline">\$\\cX\$</span> is defined as follows: <span class="math display">\\$$ H(p) = -\\int\_{\\cX} p(x) \\log p(x)\\,d\\mu(x), \\$$</span> If <span class="math inline">\$\\mu\$</span> is a finite measure, the entropy simply <span class="math inline">\$\\log\\mu(\\cX)\$</span> minus the KL divergence from <span class="math inline">\$p\$</span> to the uniform density <span class="math inline">\$p\_{\\text{unif}}(x)\\equiv \\frac{1}{\\mu(\\cX)}\$</span>: <span class="math display">\\$$ D\_{\\text{KL}}(p \\,\\\|\\, p\_{\\text{unif}}) = \\int\_{\\cX} p(x)\\log\\frac{p(x)}{\\mu(\\cX)^{-1}}\\,d\\mu(x) = \\log\\mu(\\cX)- H(p). \\$$</span> As a result, the entropy maximizing distribution is simply the uniform distribution itself. More generally suppose we impose a constraint that some statistic <span class="math inline">\$T(X) \\in \\RR^s\$</span> has expectation <span class="math inline">\$\\nu\\in \\RR^s\$</span>, i.e. <span class="math inline">\$\\EE\_p T(X) = \\nu\$</span>. If the constraint is feasible, it can be shown that the constrained maximum-entropy distribution is of the form <span class="math inline">\$p(x) = e^{\\eta'T(x)-A(\\eta)}\$</span>, where <span class="math inline">\$\\eta\$</span> is chosen to satisfy the constraint; that is, constrained entropy-maximizing distributions are exponential family distributions.

Suppose that we want our prior to have mean <span class="math inline">\$\\EE\_\\lambda\\theta = \\nu\$</span> and variance <span class="math inline">\$\\Var\_\\lambda\\theta = \\sigma^2\$</span>, but otherwise we want to remain indifferent about what functional form it should take, perhaps because we find it difficult to say what our prior beliefs about <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\$</span> are in such detail. If we choose the exponential family distribution with sufficient statistics <span class="math inline">\$S(\\theta) = (\\theta,\\theta^2)\$</span> and assign the natural parameters so <span class="math inline">\$\\EE\_\\lambda S(\\theta) = (\\nu, \\nu^2+\\sigma^2)\$</span>, we will have chosen the entropy-maximizing prior among all priors that match our mean and variance assumptions — and the prior will likely be mathematically convenient as well! In particular, if the base measure <span class="math inline">\$\\mu\$</span> is the Lebesgue measure on <span class="math inline">\$\\RR\$</span>, then our prior is exactly <span class="math inline">\$N(\\nu,\\sigma^2)\$</span>.

## <span class="header-section-number">2.5</span> Prior or Concurrent Experience {.anchored number="2.5" anchor-id="prior-or-concurrent-experience"}

The best source of a prior, when available, is prior or concurrent experience with other instances of similar statistical problems. If we assume the parameter values for those problems are drawn independently from the same prior, we can fruitfully incorporate all of the data into the model. In this case, even a frequentist might agree that it is reasonable to model the parameter values for the different problems as being drawn from a probability distribution.

There are two closely related ideas for how to learn a prior distribution from a set of similar problem instances. The more frequentist idea is called **empirical Bayes**: instead of inventing the prior, we treat it as an unknown quantity and *estimate* it from the data. The Bayesian idea, called **hierarchical Bayes**, is that we treat the parameters of the prior as additional Bayesian parameters to estimate, assign a prior to them, and learn them from the data using Bayesian inference on the full model. In practice, these two approaches can look and perform very similarly to each other.

**Example: Hierarchical Beta-binomial model**

Consider, for example, assuming that the <span class="math inline">\$m=48\$</span> coin flippers in the Bartosz et al. paper have different same-side biases <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\_1,\\ldots,\\theta\_m\$</span>, that these <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\_i\$</span> values are drawn from a common Beta distribution. <span class="math display">\\$$ \\begin{aligned} \\theta\_i &\\simiid \\text{Beta}(\\alpha,\\beta), \\quad \\text{ for } i = 1,\\ldots,m\\\\ X\_i \\mid\\theta &\\simind \\text{Binom}(n\_i, \\theta\_i) \\end{aligned} \\$$</span> We have already calculated the posterior mean of <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\_i\$</span> for fixed <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\$</span> and <span class="math inline">\$\\beta\$</span>: <span class="math display">\\$$ \\delta\_{i;\\alpha,\\beta}(X) = \\EE\[ \\theta\_i \\mid X$$ = \\frac{X\_i+\\alpha}{n\_i+\\alpha+\\beta}. \\\]</span> Note that this only depends on <span class="math inline">\$X\_i\$</span>: when <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\$</span> and <span class="math inline">\$\\beta\$</span> are fixed, the <span class="math inline">\$(\\theta\_i,X\_i)\$</span> pairs are independent, so observing <span class="math inline">\$X\_2,\\ldots,X\_m\$</span> tells us nothing about the distribution of <span class="math inline">\$(\\theta\_1,X\_1)\$</span>.

If we were solving only one problem of this type, we might select <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\$</span> and <span class="math inline">\$\\beta\$</span> to get a uniform prior (<span class="math inline">\$\\alpha=\\beta=1\$</span>), a Jeffreys prior (<span class="math inline">\$\\alpha=\\beta=1/2\$</span>), or an informative prior (<span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\$</span> pseudo-successes and <span class="math inline">\$\\beta\$</span> pseudo-failures), but if we are solving many problems at once we can learn the prior parameters.

For an empirical Bayes approach to selecting <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\$</span> and <span class="math inline">\$\\beta\$</span>, we would likely begin by observing that, after marginalizing out <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\_1,\\ldots,\\theta\_m\$</span>, we have the likelihood model: <span class="math display">\\$$ X\_i \\sim \\text{Beta-Binom}(n\_i,\\alpha,\\beta), \\$$</span> and estimate <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\$</span> and <span class="math inline">\$\\beta\$</span>, usually by maximum likelihood. Then we would plug these values in to obtain our best estimate of the optimal Bayes estimator: <span class="math display">\\$$ \\delta\_{i;\\hat\\alpha,\\hat\\beta}(X) = \\frac{X\_i + \\hat\\alpha}{n\_i + \\hat\\alpha+\\hat\\beta}. \\$$</span>

The hierarchical Bayes approach is that we assign them to have a common **hyperprior** <span class="math inline">\$\\lambda\_0\$</span>:

<span class="math display">\\$$ \\begin{aligned} \\alpha,\\beta&\\sim \\lambda\_0(\\alpha,\\beta)\\\\ \\theta\_i \\mid \\alpha,\\beta &\\simiid \\text{Beta}(\\alpha,\\beta), \\quad \\text{ for } i = 1,\\ldots,m\\\\ X\_i \\mid \\alpha,\\beta,\\theta &\\simind \\text{Binom}(n\_i, \\theta\_i) \\end{aligned} \\$$</span> Again, if we condition on <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha\$</span> and <span class="math inline">\$\\beta\$</span>, the problems have nothing to do with each other. But observe what happens when the parameters <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha,\\beta\$</span> are unknown:

<span class="math display">\\$$ \\begin{aligned} \\delta\_i(X) &= \\EE\[\\theta\_i \\mid X$$\\\\ &= \\EE\\left$$ \\; \\EE\[\\theta\_i \\mid X, \\alpha, \\beta$$ \\mid X\\;\\right\] \\\\ &= \\EE\\left$$ \\; \\frac{X\_i+\\alpha}{n\_i+\\alpha+\\beta} \\mid X\\;\\right$$ \\\\ &= \\int\_{\\alpha,\\beta} \\frac{X\_i+\\alpha}{n\_i+\\alpha+\\beta} \\,d\\lambda(\\alpha,\\beta \\mid X). \\end{aligned} \\\]</span> That is, our final estimator is a *mixture* over estimators of the form <span class="math inline">\$\\frac{X\_i+\\alpha}{n\_i+\\alpha+\\beta}\$</span>, where <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha,\\beta\$</span> are sampled from their joint posterior distribution after seeing all of the data. Thus, in a sense we have learned from the full data set how to estimate <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\_i\$</span> from <span class="math inline">\$X\_i\$</span>.

If <span class="math inline">\$m\$</span> is large, then the amount of data we have all together may be much greater than the amount of data we have for each problem instance. Then we potentially have the best of both worlds: an informative prior that helps us better estimate each <span class="math inline">\$\\theta\_i\$</span>, but strong intersubjective agreement about *which* hyperparameters <span class="math inline">\$\\alpha,\\beta\$</span> we should use for that prior.

[^1]: Note that Bayesians often retort that coming up with the likelihood is no less problematic because it also commonly involves a great deal of subjective judgment — this is certainly true in some cases (and less true in others), but at least we usually sample repeatedly from the likelihood so we can usually check assumptions like independence of successive observations, normality of errors, and so on.
