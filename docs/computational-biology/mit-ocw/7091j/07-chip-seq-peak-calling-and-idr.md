# 7. ChIP-seq Peak Calling and IDR

## What this covers

ChIP-seq tells you, genome-wide, where a transcription factor is likely bound — but only
indirectly, through a cloud of short sequencing reads scattered around the true binding site. This
chapter covers how to turn that cloud into a trustworthy list of binding events: a probabilistic
model of where a single event's reads land, the EM algorithm used to fit a sparse mixture of such
events across an entire genome (GPS), a significance test that uses a matched control experiment to
reject noise, and the Irreproducible Discovery Rate (IDR), which decides how many events to keep by
checking consistency across replicates rather than any absolute score. It assumes the previous two
lectures — aligning high-throughput reads to a genome, and assembling a genome from reads — but
builds the probability model, the EM derivation and the hypothesis tests from scratch.

## What a ChIP-seq experiment measures

Transcription factors are proteins — roughly 2,000 of them in humans — that bind specific DNA
sequences and act as molecular switches: a DNA-binding domain recognizes the sequence, and an
activation or repression domain recruits (or blocks) RNA polymerase, sometimes by folding the
genome to bring distant regulatory sequences together. Finding out which genes a given regulator
controls, and precisely where on the genome it sits while doing so, is one of the central
techniques for understanding gene regulation. Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing
(ChIP-seq) is the standard way to do this, one protein at a time.

The protocol:

1. **Crosslink** proteins to DNA in living cells — a chemical "flash photograph" that freezes every
   protein, not just the one of interest, onto the genome.
2. **Harvest and fragment** the chromatin, typically by sonication, which breaks the DNA at
   essentially random positions (fragments of roughly 200–300 bp).
3. **Immunoprecipitate** with an antibody against the protein of interest, or against an epitope tag
   engineered onto it, to enrich for the fragments that protein was sitting on.
4. **Reverse the crosslinks**, amplify, and sequence — typically short reads (around 35 bp) from one
   end of each fragment.
5. In parallel, sequence the **whole-cell extract (WCE)** — the same chromatin, fragmented the same
   way, but *without* immunoprecipitation — as a control.

A sequencer always reads 5′ to 3′, so what is actually recorded for each fragment is the position of
its 5′ end, on whichever strand it came from — not full fragment coverage, and not a count of how
many times a base is sequenced. A read is counted once, at the position where it starts. Reads
generated by a single binding event therefore form two offset distributions: forward-strand reads
pile up just upstream of the site, reverse-strand reads just downstream, with a dip at the site
itself, where the crosslinked protein blocked fragmentation and sequencing. With on the order of 10
million cells and a good antibody, this resolves binding locations to roughly 10 bp genome-wide — a
spatial resolution that was not achievable even a decade before this lecture.

## Why peak-finding alone is not enough

The naive approach is **peak-finding**: smooth the read density along the genome and report each
local maximum as a binding event. This works when binding events are far apart, but as two events
move closer together their read distributions overlap, and the two peaks merge into a single broad
peak. The reads observed at such a locus are, to good approximation, the *sum* of the two
individual per-event distributions — this additivity holds because the experiment pools millions of
cells, not all of which have both sites occupied simultaneously, so the measured signal is an
average over many independent realizations. Recovering two (or more) component events from their
sum — so-called **homotypic** binding events, closely spaced copies of the same motif — is exactly
the problem a peak-finder cannot solve and a generative model can.

## The spatial distribution of reads around one event

GPS (the algorithm this lecture builds up — "genome positioning system") starts by characterizing
the shape a single binding event produces. For a read at position $r_i$ caused by a binding event at
position $b_j$:

$$p(r_i \mid b_j) = p(r_i \mid z_{ij}=1) = \text{emp}\big((-1)^{S_i}(r_i - b_j)\big)$$

where $S_i = 0$ for a forward-strand read and $S_i=1$ for a reverse-strand read, and $\text{emp}(d)$
is the *empirical* spatial distribution of reads at offset $d$ from a binding site — a histogram
built directly from data, not a fitted analytic density, over a window of a few hundred base pairs.
For simplicity the model assumes the forward- and reverse-strand distributions are mirror images of
each other, so only one needs to be learned and then flipped for the other strand. Because this
distribution varies from protein to protein, the plan is to learn it from the data itself and
re-learn it as better event calls become available: run the algorithm once with a generic
distribution, use the resulting confident events to build a better empirical distribution, and
re-run.

## GPS: a genome-wide mixture model at single-base resolution

The key move is to model **every base in the genome** as a candidate center of a binding event, and
let the data decide which candidates actually have mass. Let $\pi$ be a vector with one entry per
genome position $m = 1, \dots, M$, where $\pi_m$ is the probability (the "strength") of a binding
event at position $m$, subject to $\sum_m \pi_m = 1$. If there are really only two true binding
events, the ideal solution has $\pi_{m_1} = \pi_{m_2} = 0.5$ and zero everywhere else — the
constraint doesn't say only one event happened, it says there is exactly one unit of probability
mass to be distributed over all the positions that, together, explain the reads.

Given $\pi$, the probability of a single observed read $r_n$ is a mixture over every possible
originating position:

$$p(r_n \mid \pi) = \sum_{m=1}^M \pi_m\, p(r_n \mid m)$$

and the likelihood of the whole read set $R = \{r_1, \dots, r_N\}$, assuming reads are generated
independently, is the product over reads:

$$p(R \mid \pi) = \prod_{n=1}^N \sum_{m=1}^M \pi_m\, p(r_n \mid m), \qquad \sum_{m=1}^M \pi_m = 1$$

The number of reads $N$ is typically $10$–$20$ million; the number of positions $M$ is the size of
the genome (billions); the true number of binding events is typically $5$–$30{,}000$ — many orders
of magnitude smaller than $M$, which is exactly why a method that favors a *sparse* $\pi$ is the
right kind of model, well before any prior is written down. Fitting $\pi$ by maximum likelihood,
$\hat\pi = \arg\max_\pi p(R \mid \pi)$, would in principle solve the whole problem — but the sum
inside the product makes direct maximization intractable, which is where EM comes in.

### The EM derivation: if we knew the assignment, this would be trivial

Introduce a latent indicator $g(z_n = m)$: 1 if read $n$ was generated by the event at $m$, 0
otherwise. If $g$ were known, counting binding strength would be immediate: the number of reads
attributable to the event at $m$ is just

$$N_m = \sum_{n=1}^N g(z_n = m),$$

and the maximum-likelihood mixing weight is simply the fraction of all reads assigned to $m$,
$\hat\pi_m = N_m / \sum_{m'} N_{m'}$. The entire problem is choosing where each read "belongs" — and
that choice is latent. EM exploits the fact that a good guess for $g$ gives a good estimate of
$\pi$, and a good estimate of $\pi$ in turn gives a better guess for $g$, and alternates between the
two:

**E step** — given the current $\pi$, compute $\gamma(z_n=m)$, the *soft*, fractional assignment of
read $n$ to event $m$:

$$\gamma(z_n = m) = \frac{\pi_m\, p(r_n \mid m)}{\sum_{m'=1}^M \pi_{m'}\, p(r_n \mid m')}$$

**M step** — given $\gamma$, recompute the effective read counts and update $\pi$ exactly as if
$\gamma$ were the true (soft) assignment:

$$N_m = \sum_{n=1}^N \gamma(z_n = m), \qquad \hat\pi_m^{(i)} = \frac{N_m}{\sum_{m'=1}^M N_{m'}}$$

$\pi$ is initialized uniformly, $\pi_m = 1/M$, and the two steps alternate until convergence; the
final $N_m$ — the effective number of reads assigned to position $m$ — is reported as the strength
of the binding event there. (Because each event's strength depends only on reads near it, the
genome can be chopped into independent pieces and processed in parallel.) Like any EM run, the
result is a local optimum that depends on the starting point, not a guaranteed global one.

### Without a prior, the result is a smear

Run as just described, on synthetic data with two planted events 50 bp apart, the algorithm
distributes $\pi$'s probability mass across a wide, fuzzy range of neighboring positions rather than
collapsing it onto the two true locations — and the same happens on real Oct4 ChIP-seq data, where
mass leaks out even into positions with only background noise. The model is not wrong, but nothing
in it *prefers* a solution where most positions get exactly zero.

### A sparsity prior forces component elimination

The fix is to add a prior that actively penalizes nonzero $\pi_m$ — a negative Dirichlet-type prior:

$$p(\pi) \propto \prod_{m=1}^M \frac{1}{(\pi_m)^\alpha}, \qquad \alpha > 0$$

which grows rapidly as $\pi_m \to 0$, so the posterior favors driving most components exactly to
zero. Maximizing the posterior (now a MAP rather than ML estimate) leaves the E step unchanged, but
changes the M step to:

$$\hat\pi_m^{(i)} = \frac{\max(0,\, N_m - \alpha)}{\sum_{m'=1}^M \max(0,\, N_{m'} - \alpha)}$$

Subtracting $\alpha$ from every component's read count means: if a candidate event doesn't have at
least $\alpha$ reads of support, its count is clipped to zero and it is permanently eliminated —
**component elimination**. The algorithm doesn't eliminate everything below threshold immediately;
weak components are allowed to accumulate support over a few iterations before being zeroed, so that
real but initially under-supported events aren't killed prematurely.

With this prior, the same synthetic two-event example converges cleanly onto exactly the two planted
positions, with the rest of $\pi$ at zero. On the real Oct4 data, the smear collapses into a handful
of sharp components around the true peaks. And on closely-spaced CTCF homotypic events — where a
single broad pile of reads conceals two adjacent binding sites — the sparse-prior EM recovers both
locations separately, where methods that assume "one pile of reads = one binding event" cannot.

## Is an event real? A binomial test against the control channel

Not every pile of reads is a real binding event. Genome assembly (an earlier lecture's subject)
cannot always resolve the true copy number of repeat elements; when repeats are collapsed, reads
originating from many genomic copies all map to the one assembled copy, producing a "tower" of reads
that looks exactly like strong, narrow enrichment but is purely an assembly artifact.

This is why the whole-cell extract control exists. The approach is a two-step process:

1. Run the GPS discovery pipeline on the IP channel alone, ignoring the control, to get a candidate
   list of events and their strengths $N_m$.
2. Filter that list using the control channel: at each candidate event, ask whether the IP read count
   is significantly larger than the control read count would allow under a null model of pure chance.

The null model treats reads at a given locus, pooled across the IP and control channels, as the
outcome of a fair coin flip: each read is equally likely (under the null of no real binding) to have
landed in either channel. This equal-likelihood assumption is only valid once the channels' overall
read budgets are matched — since the IP channel spends many of its reads on real enrichment while
the control does not, the control counts are first rescaled using regions of the genome known to be
free of events, matching IP and control read density there before comparing at a candidate event.
Given the (scaled) control count $k$ and total pooled count $n$ at a candidate locus, the one-sided
binomial tail probability is:

$$F(k, n, P) = \sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor k \rfloor} \binom{n}{l} P^l (1-P)^{n-l}, \qquad P = 0.5$$

— the probability, under the null, of seeing as few as $k$ (scaled) control reads out of $n$ total.
A small $F$ rejects the null and supports calling the locus a real event.

## Multiple testing: Benjamini–Hochberg at a desired FDR

A genome-wide scan produces p-values for tens or hundreds of thousands of candidate events
simultaneously, so a fixed p-value cutoff is not principled on its own — some fraction of candidates
will pass by chance no matter how small the cutoff, purely because so many are tested. The
Benjamini–Hochberg procedure controls this directly: sort all candidate events by p-value, smallest
to largest (rank $1$ = most significant), and assign each a $Q$-value,

$$Q\text{-value} = P\text{-value} \times \frac{\text{Count}}{\text{Rank}}$$

where $\text{Count}$ is the total number of events tested. Accept events at ranks $1, \dots, k$ up to
the largest $k$ for which the $Q$-value is still below the desired false discovery rate (FDR) —
commonly $0.05$. The FDR has a direct interpretation: if $1{,}000$ events are called at FDR $0.1$,
roughly $100$ of them are expected to be false positives — it is the *fraction* of the accepted list
expected to be wrong, not a per-event significance threshold.

## Are two replicates consistent? The Irreproducible Discovery Rate

Every real ChIP-seq experiment should be done at least twice. Given two ranked lists of events from
two replicate experiments — ranked from most to least significant, with matching events identified
by shared genomic location — the question is how far down the two lists agreement can be trusted.

### Spearman rank correlation: necessary, not sufficient

A natural first idea is to correlate the two experiments' rankings. The Spearman rank correlation is
the ordinary (Pearson) correlation computed on *ranks* rather than raw scores:

$$\rho = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2}}$$

where $x_i, y_i$ are the ranks of matched event $i$ in lists $X$ and $Y$. This is useful precisely
because it does not depend on the specific numeric scores each experiment happens to produce — only
on their order — so two experiments whose raw scores correlate only loosely (say, a Pearson
correlation of $0.88$) can still be in perfect rank agreement ($\rho = 1$). But a single correlation
coefficient summarizes the *whole* list at once; it cannot say where, within the list, agreement
breaks down — and that breakdown point is exactly what is needed to decide how many events to keep.

### The correspondence curve

Define $\Psi_n(t)$ as the fraction of the top $n \cdot t$ events (by rank) that appear in *both*
lists' top $n \cdot t$ — i.e., how well the two replicates agree once you restrict to the top
fraction $t$ of the ranking. In the idealized case where the top half of all $n$ events are genuine
signal, perfectly concordant between replicates, and the bottom half is pure noise with no
concordance at all, $\Psi_n(t)$ rises with roughly unit slope up to $t = 0.5$ (every additional event
admitted is a true positive, matched in both replicates) and then flattens — additional entries past
that point are noise, matched only by chance. The derivative $\Psi_n'(t)$ makes the transition
sharper to see: high while still in the reproducible region, dropping once the ranking crosses into
noise.

### Fitting the break point: a two-component mixture

Real data isn't a clean two-piece idealization, so IDR models the paired ranks directly as a mixture
of two components — a "reproducible" component and an "irreproducible" component — and fits the
mixture's parameters from the observed pairs. Given a desired irreproducible discovery rate $\alpha$,
IDR selects the top $\ell$ pairs such that the (fitted) probability that a selected pair actually
belongs to the irreproducible component is $\alpha$. This is how the ENCODE pipeline uses it in
practice: each replicate is processed independently through event discovery, both resulting event
lists are ranked by significance, and IDR is applied to the pair of rankings to decide how far down
each list to trust. Different peak-calling methods reach IDR $= 0.05$ after retaining very different
numbers of peaks — some run out of reproducible signal quickly, others can go much further down
their own ranked list before hitting the bound.

### Consistency is not correctness

A caution worth holding onto: an algorithm that is *consistently wrong* — for example, one that
always calls the same assembly-repeat "towers" as top events, in the same order, every time it is
run — will score perfectly by IDR, because its answers are reproducible, not because they are
correct. Reproducibility is necessary but not sufficient for validity. A useful sanity check is to
run IDR on a pairing that should *not* agree — an experiment against a mismatched or unrelated
dataset — and confirm that it correctly fails to find reproducible structure; a method that reports
high agreement even there is not to be trusted on the real comparison either.

## Pushing resolution further: motifs as a positional prior (GEM)

The lecture closes by pointing toward an extension built on exactly the same machinery: once you can
resolve binding to within roughly 20 bp genome-wide, you can ask whether candidate binding events sit
near known or discovered sequence motifs, and use that as evidence. GEM couples binding-event finding
to motif discovery in both directions — biasing discovered motifs toward predicted binding sites, and
biasing binding-event predictions toward motif positions — by replacing the uniform sparsity prior
with a **position-specific** prior:

$$p(\pi) \propto \prod_{m=1}^M (\pi_m)^{-\alpha_s + \alpha_m}$$

where $\alpha_s > 0$ is the same uniform sparseness parameter as before, and $\alpha_m$ adds extra
weight at positions where a motif has been found, making the model more tolerant of a weak read-count
event there. The payoff the lecture gestures at — resolving binding events to the point of computing,
genome-wide, the significant spacings between pairs of profiled transcription factors — is this same
analysis: once events are localized this precisely, you can start reading off the regulatory grammar
the genome actually uses (for instance, a substantial fraction of Oct4 sites sitting within 100 bp of
a Sox2 site, suggesting cooperative or co-occurring binding).
